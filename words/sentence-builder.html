The block system文の組み立て

Build a sentence in chunks, in this order. The particle belongs to the chunk before it and tells you what that chunk is doing — which is why Japanese works without spaces, and why the chunks can be reordered without the meaning moving.

WHO / TOPICは · がTIMEにPLACEで · に · へ · から · までHOW / WITHと · でWHATをVERB / です—QUESTIONか
WHO / TOPICは · が
The thing the sentence is about. は sets the topic, が picks out the subject.
TIMEに
に for a specific clock time or date. No に on あした, きょう, まいにち, いま.
PLACEで · に · へ · から · まで
で where an action happens · に destination or existence · へ direction · から from · まで as far as.
HOW / WITHと · で
と with a person · で by means of. Plain adverbs (すこし, もう一度) sit here and take no particle.
WHATを
The direct object. Adjectives attach in front of the noun, inside this block.
VERB / です—
ます for actions, です for descriptions. Always last, before か.
QUESTIONか
Turns the finished sentence into a question. Nothing follows it.

How this connects to the kanji

The three scripts split the work, and that split is what makes an unspaced sentence readable. Kanji carry the content: nouns, and the stem of every verb and adjective. Hiragana carry the grammar: every particle in the chart above, plus the okurigana tails on 飲みます and 高い. Katakana carry loanwords: ビール, メニュー, チェックイン.

So a particle is never written in kanji. When you see a lone hiragana character sitting between two kanji words, it is almost always doing a grammatical job — and the colour coding below is showing you which one.

私はカフェでコーヒーを飲みました  — kanji for 私, 飲; katakana for カフェ, コーヒー; hiragana for は, で, を, みました.

Nothing matches that. Try an English word, a kanji, or a rōmaji fragment like onegai.

Verbs and adjectives: types and forms動詞・形容詞

The block system tells you where the verb goes. This tells you what to do with it. Every N5 and N4 verb and adjective, sorted by type, with the forms you need most. Traps are listed first in each group.

う or る? A verb ending in る is a る-verb only if the sound before る is an i or e sound (食べる, 見る). Everything else is an う-verb. The exceptions look like る-verbs but aren't: 帰る, 入る, 切る, 知る, 走る.
Pairs that trade particles. Verbs you do to something take を: 窓を開ける (I open the window). Their partners that happen on their own take が: 窓が開く (the window opens). The same goes for 閉める and 閉まる.
Particles that surprise. 好き, 嫌い, 分かる and 欲しい take が, not を. 乗る takes に (電車に乗る), and 降りる takes を (電車を降りる).

Reading the colours, and one caveat

A colour is a job, not a position. Pink is always whatever the sentence is about, orange is always the thing being acted on, purple is always the predicate. English puts the purple block second; Japanese puts it last, every time without exception. In 66 of these 120 sentences the two languages order the blocks differently, and the Order strip under each one shows the two sequences side by side so you can see exactly which blocks moved.

The six buttons are independent. English alone is a production test — say it, then switch kanji back on. Kanji alone is a reading test. Kana plus rōmaji is what you want if you are still building reading speed. Colour can be turned off once the block order feels automatic, which is the point at which you have actually learnt it.

The caveat: the block order is a scaffold, not a law. Japanese word order is genuinely flexible, because the particles — not the positions — carry the grammar. WHO and TIME swap freely, and in real speech whole blocks get dropped the moment they are obvious, which is why half the sentences here have no 私は in them at all. What you cannot move is the verb: it goes last, with か after it. Treat the order as the safe default that is never wrong, not as the only thing that is right.

Everything here is polite ます／です form. That is the right register for shops, stations, restaurants and people you have just met, and the wrong one for close friends — but it is never rude, which plain form can be.
