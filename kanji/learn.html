漢字 学習

Learn the kanji from N5 up to N2 (1,086 in all), and their words, the way the best teachers and the memory research agree on: building blocks first, one vivid story per kanji, one real word for the readings, and spaced-repetition flashcards that schedule every review for you.

The short answer

The fastest reliable route to the N5 and N4 kanji is about 7 weeks at 25 to 35 minutes a day: 10 new kanji a day for 29 days, with the flashcard reviews carrying on for another 3 to 4 weeks until they stick. You can halve the introduction phase by doing 20 a day, but the daily reviews roughly double, to about an hour. Going faster than that mostly buys forgetting.

That pace works because it combines five things that teachers who get results (Heisig's Remembering the Kanji, WaniKani and Tofugu, and the Anki community) all share, and that the memory research backs up.

The five principles

1. Learn the building blocks before the kanji

Kanji are not 284 separate pictures. Most are two or three familiar parts. Once 木 (tree) and 亻 (person) are known, 休 (rest) is "a person leaning on a tree" rather than seven random strokes. This deck orders every kanji so its parts always come first, starting with the twelve master shapes (人 口 日 木 水 手 心 言 糸 門 辶 力) that between them sit inside well over 100 of the 284.

2. One vivid story per kanji

Tie the parts together with a short, concrete, slightly odd image. This is the keyword method, and it's the best-supported mnemonic technique in the research (it goes back to Atkinson and Raugh's vocabulary studies in the 1970s). Every card has a story, but Heisig's advice stands: if yours is more vivid, overwrite it, because a story you invent sticks better than one you're handed.

3. Meaning first, then one real word for the readings

Don't memorise reading lists. Each kanji first gets a meaning card (kanji → keyword and story). Once you know the meaning, a word card unlocks: one common word that carries the kanji's main reading, such as 食堂 for しょく or 食べる for た. The JLPT tests words in sentences, never reading lists, and a word gives the reading something to hang on. The colours tell you which kind of reading you're looking at: indigo for on (from Chinese, mostly in compounds), green for kun (native Japanese, mostly kanji plus hiragana), and red dotted for a special whole-word reading.

4. Test yourself instead of re-reading

Looking at a card and thinking "yes, I know that" does very little. Trying to recall the answer before you flip, even when you fail, is what builds the memory. This is the testing effect, one of the most replicated findings in learning science (Roediger and Karpicke, 2006). So always say the keyword, or the reading, out loud before you tap Show answer.

5. Space the reviews, and never skip a day's reviews

Each time you remember a card, the gap before you see it again grows: 1 day, 3 days, a week, three weeks and beyond. Reviewing just as you're about to forget is far more efficient than cramming (the spacing effect, first shown by Ebbinghaus and confirmed by large reviews such as Cepeda and colleagues in 2006). The scheduler here does the timing. Your only job is to clear what's due each day. If you miss a day, do your reviews before any new cards.

Your daily routine

  1. Reviews first (10 to 20 minutes). Open Study and clear everything due. Grade honestly: Again if you didn't get it, Hard if it took a real struggle, Good if you got it, Easy only if it was instant.
  2. New kanji (10 to 15 minutes). The same session then brings in today's new cards in order. For each one, read the parts and story slowly, picture the scene, and check the look-alikes. Those are what cost marks.
  3. Use them (5 minutes, optional but powerful). Read a few sentences in the sentence builder or the phrase guide and spot today's kanji. Seeing a kanji in real text is what turns "I recognise it" into "I can read it".

What to skip if speed matters

Handwriting. Writing kanji by hand helps some people remember detail, but it's slow, and the JLPT only tests recognition. For the shortest route, trace a new kanji once with your finger to notice its parts, then move on. Stroke order can wait until you want to write. Every reading of every kanji: learn the one or two in the word card, and pick up the rest as you meet new words.

How the other files fit in

This file is the engine: the order, the stories and the scheduler. The others are for looking things up and for practice. Use the Heisig deck to read a whole family at once, the kanji by shape, sound and topic file to see patterns such as sound parts (寺 gives ジ in 時 and 持), the kana sounds file for the reading traps, and the sentence builder and phrase guide to see your kanji in real sentences.

A note on the evidence

Spacing, retrieval practice and keyword mnemonics are among the most reliable findings in the psychology of learning. The specific schedule here (1 and 10 minute learning steps, then intervals that grow by about 2.5 times each success) follows Anki's widely used version of the SM-2 algorithm, simplified. The time estimates are typical, not guaranteed. Your pace depends on your honesty with the grading buttons and on not skipping days.
