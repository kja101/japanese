Kanji by Situation

The same stories as the shape deck, resorted by where you will actually use them: the restaurant, the bar, the pharmacy, the station, the office. Within each topic, kanji are grouped by whether their anchor word is a noun, a verb or an adjective.

Each anchor word is coloured by reading type: indigo underline for an on reading (from Chinese), green for a kun reading (native Japanese), red dotted for a special whole-word reading. The tag under each big kanji says which reading it takes in its anchor word.

Every entry carries a real word rather than an abstract keyword, and a line saying where in Japan you will actually run into it — on a menu, a departure board, a shop curtain, a clinic door.

Reading the breakdowns

Every component below its glyph carries a tag saying what it actually is. Only two of the five are worth studying.

  • kanjiA real kanji that is a word on its own. Learn it — you get a second character free.
  • kanji + kanaA real kanji, but it only becomes a word with hiragana attached (占う, 高い). Learn the kanji and its tail together.
  • compounds onlyA real kanji you will never see standing alone — it lives inside compounds like 未来 or 台所.
  • rareA genuine kanji, but well beyond N4. Recognise the shape as scaffolding and move on.
  • radicalNot a kanji at all. A positional shape with no reading and no standalone use — 广, 亻, 氵. Recognise, never study.
Nothing matches that. Try the kanji itself, a kana reading, or an English word.

One thing this organisation gets wrong, and how to read around it

Kanji do not have parts of speech. Words do. 食 is a noun in 食べ物 and a verb in 食べる, and nothing about the character decides which. So the noun/verb/adjective split here is a split by anchor word — the single most useful word built on that kanji, chosen for the topic it sits in. It is a filing decision, not a fact about the character, and you should expect to meet most of these kanji in all three roles eventually.

The same goes for topics. 場 is filed under the city because you meet it on car-park signs, but it is equally at home in 会場 and 工場. A kanji appears exactly once here so that the deck stays a complete checklist; the cross-references in the "where you meet it" lines are there to stop that filing from misleading you.

Coverage is the standard community N4 list plus about fifteen N5 characters the topic vocabulary genuinely needs — you cannot do a restaurant chapter without 飲 or a body chapter without 足. JLPT has published no official kanji list since 2010, so the level boundary is approximate either way.

Stories are carried over unchanged from the shape deck so the two agree. They are memory devices, not etymology, and the best version of any of them is the one you rewrite yourself.

Use the two decks differently: the shape deck to learn how kanji are built, this one to revise before you need to walk into somewhere and read something. Search filters every topic at once; Test me blanks the meanings and stories so you can tap only what you cannot recall.
